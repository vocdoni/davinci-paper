\subsection{Security discussion}
\label{sec:analysis:security}

Based on the above principles, the protocol provides a number of concrete security properties that ensure the integrity, confidentiality, and verifiability of voting events. In what follows we discuss how \davinci achieves these properties, with particular emphasis on integrity, ballot secrecy, and the obfuscation of revotes, before turning to data availability, liveness, verifiability, and robustness against quantum threats. The discussion is informal: it states what each mechanism enforces and under which assumptions, but it does not prove the properties in a formal model, such as a receipt-freeness or coercion-resistance game. Table~\ref{tab:trust} summarizes the assumptions that each property relies on.

\paragraph{Integrity and soundness.} The integrity of the election rests on the soundness of the proofs rather than on trust in any operator: the knowledge soundness of Groth16 for ballot proofs, and the soundness of the ZisK \acp{STARK} and of the PlonK wrapper for the batch and results programs. No party (including a malicious sequencer) can advance the state from $S_i$ to $S_{i+1}$ without supplying a valid batch proof, since the registry rejects any update not accompanied by such a proof. Because a \ac{zkVM} proves the execution of a program, the statement that is proven is exactly what the program checks, and the registry pins the program through its verification key: an update proven by any other program, under any other setup, or with a failed verdict ($\okflag \ne 1$) is rejected. The registry also binds every proof to the election: the old root must be the latest root, the census root must be one the election accepts, the count of occupied slots must match, and the blob openings must match the published blobs. Consequently, every counted ballot carries a valid membership proof in a census the election accepts, and every accumulator update follows the protocol rules. Double voting is prevented \emph{without} nullifiers: each voter is bound to a slot in the state tree, so a second submission by the same voter overwrites their previous ballot at that slot rather than adding a new contribution to the tally. This one-slot-per-voter semantics relies on invariants of the census that the batch program checks only within a batch: in a Merkle census, no two members may share a slot; in a \ac{CSP} census, the \ac{CSP} must assign one stable index to each voter and distinct indexes to distinct voters (see Section~\ref{sec:vocdoni-protocol:census}). Integrity is therefore relative to the configured census and to these invariants; whether the census itself lists the right people and weights is outside the protocol. Vote identifiers, by contrast, are freshly derived for every ballot, subject to collision rejection and resampling (Section~\ref{sec:vocdoni-protocol:state-tree}), which enables individual verifiability without revealing the slot of the voter. The guest programs are built reproducibly, so anyone can recompile the published source, recompute the program keys, and compare them with the keys pinned by the registry.

\paragraph{Obfuscation of revotes.} \Davinci aims to prevent voters from proving to others how they voted, which mitigates coercion and vote-buying, by combining re-encryption, ballot overwrites, and randomized state updates.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textit{Ballot re-encryption}: since our encryption scheme supports re-randomization, that is, a ciphertext can be refreshed with new randomness without changing the underlying message, sequencers re-encrypt the received ballots before committing them to the state Merkle tree. Under the decisional Diffie--Hellman (DDH) assumption on BabyJubjub, a re-randomized ciphertext is indistinguishable from a fresh encryption of the same message, so the stored ciphertext cannot be matched with the submitted one by its contents. This does not hide which slot was written: with a Merkle census, slots are a public function of addresses. The re-encryption scalars are derived inside the batch program from a single secret seed per batch and the old state root, and every active ciphertext consumes the next position of the scalar chain, so no position is ever used twice; distinct positions yield equal scalars only with negligible probability.
	\item \textit{Handling receipts}: because ballots are re-randomized, a voter cannot use the randomness $k$ of their encryption as a receipt, since the ciphertext on-chain no longer corresponds to it.
	\item \textit{Overwrites}: voters may cast a new ballot while the voting period is open, replacing their earlier submission. A coerced voter can therefore change their vote afterwards, provided that they can submit unobserved and that the new ballot settles before the grace window closes. When an overwrite occurs, the batch subtracts the previous ballot and adds the new one to the results accumulator, and re-encrypts the updated ballot before storing it in the state tree.
	\item \textit{Concealing overwrites (silent revoting)}: to make overwrites hard to distinguish from routine re-randomizations, every batch also re-encrypts a sample of the occupied slots it does not write. The batch program enforces the size of the sample, at least $\min(\mathsf{target}(n, w), o - w)$ slots, with $\mathsf{target}(n, w) = \min(2048, \max(16, 2w, n))$ and $o$ the number of occupied slots, and the blobs list every updated slot in a single sorted list, so the published data does not mark which updates are overwrites. Sequencers keep a history of the batches that carried each vote, so that a vote sealed again after a lost race or a failed settlement refreshes every slot of its earlier batches that the sequencer has observed.
\end{itemize}
These mechanisms obscure revotes from observers of the public data, but the protection is conditional and partial. First, the batch program checks the size and the validity of the refresh sample, not that it was drawn uniformly or that the seed is unpredictable: the sealing sequencer and its prover, who also learn which updates are overwrites, must draw both from fresh randomness and keep them secret, and a voter who fears coercion should revote through a sequencer they trust. Second, public data still reveals participation (the first write to a slot is distinguishable, since refreshes only touch occupied slots), the number of overwrites of each batch, the set of updated slots, and the timing of batches; when few slots are occupied, the sample is small, and in the extreme cases where every occupied slot is overwritten ($w = o$, for instance a single occupied slot that is overwritten), the published data reveals the revotes directly. Third, the coverage history only includes batches that the sequencer observed: a batch of another sequencer that a reorganization removed before its blobs were fetched cannot be covered. Finally, re-encryption scalars are 256-bit SHA-256 digests reduced modulo the 254-bit order $s$ of the BN254 scalar field, which leaves them at statistical distance about $2^{-4.7}$ from uniform in $[0, s)$; after the implicit reduction modulo the subgroup order $t \approx s/8$, the scalars actually applied are at statistical distance about $2^{-7.6}$ from uniform. Removing this gap requires reducing a wider digest, and hence a new program key. We therefore claim that \davinci obscures revotes from outside observers under these conditions; we do not claim receipt-freeness or coercion resistance in a formal sense.

\paragraph{Privacy.} Ballots remain secret even though encrypted ballots are publicly stored and processed. Ballot secrecy is preserved through ElGamal encryption, which allows votes to be aggregated without decryption, and it is claimed only beyond what the published tally and metadata reveal: in a small electorate, or when tallies of overlapping elections are combined, the tally itself may reveal individual choices. When the encryption key is generated through a \ac{DKG} or a council ceremony, no single party holds the secret key, and the protocol only requests the threshold decryption of the final aggregate, never of individual ballots. Under the assumption that fewer than $\theta$ of the $\nu$ \keywardens are compromised over the whole lifetime of the key, no individual ballot ciphertext can be decrypted. A coalition of $\theta$ \keywardens, however, could decrypt every ballot published in the blobs, so the threshold must be chosen to reflect the trust placed in the committee; in the locked \ac{DKG} mode, the coalition would also need the organizer's secret. A colluding threshold can bypass policy-only gates, such as the opening date of a council, which constrain the contracts and honest members; in the locked mode, it additionally needs the organizer's secret or the organizer's cooperation, on-chain or off-chain. In the council mode, all the processes bound to a ceremony share its key and form a single privacy domain: their confidentiality additionally requires that every adapter the ceremony allows only requests the decryption of authenticated final accumulators, that ballots are encrypted with fresh randomness and proven valid, and that the setups of the council's proofs are sound~\cite{davinci-council}. Elections that need strict isolation should use separate ceremonies. In the sequencer mode, the sequencer that holds the key can decrypt every ballot, which is why this mode is reserved for elections that accept that trust. Note that, while the content of a ballot stays hidden, the protocol does reveal whether a given voter has cast a vote, that is, turnout is public, but the choice is not. With a Merkle census, the slot of a voter is a public function of their address, so the participation of each census member can be read from the state; a sequencer additionally learns the address and the submission time of every ballot it receives. In a \ac{CSP} census, the \ac{CSP} is trusted for eligibility: it can issue credentials to anyone, and it knows which index belongs to which voter.

\paragraph{Data availability.} Ballots and state updates are published in Ethereum blobs, and the registry checks, at settlement, that the published blobs open to the values the batch program derived from the verified state. A sequencer therefore cannot settle a transition whose data it withholds. In L1-blob mode, the guarantee is the same: the blobs live on Ethereum, and the registry on the rollup verifies their inclusion in the Ethereum chain from block headers anchored at the rollup's view of Ethereum, so it relies, in addition, on the rollup deriving its L1 origin correctly, an assumption the rollup itself already makes. Since blobs are pruned after about 18 days, long-term availability requires archival. The blob encoding allows any party holding a complete archive of an election's blobs to rebuild it, and every sequencer stores and serves the blobs of the transitions it has seen; the current node, however, does not yet bootstrap automatically from the archive of another node, so a node that starts after the blobs have been pruned needs its operator to provide an archival blob source. Since EIP-7594, only nodes that custody full blobs can serve them, so sequencers should rely on such nodes or on archivers.

\paragraph{Liveness and censorship resistance.} Sequencing is permissionless, so a voter whose ballot is ignored by one sequencer can submit it to another, and anyone can run a sequencer for any election. This guarantees an alternative, not fair inclusion: settlement is a race in which the first valid transition wins, and a sequencer whose batches keep losing races to transitions that omit a ballot cannot force its inclusion. The grace window lets ballots received before the end of the voting period settle after it, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:grace-end} bounds how long transitions may keep settling; it does not guarantee that every admitted ballot settles. Inclusion requires enough proving and settlement capacity, fees, and timely transaction inclusion before the window closes; ballots still queued when it closes are discarded, and voters can detect this through the status of their vote identifiers. Once results are requested from the \keywardens, the process leaves the organizer's control and can no longer be canceled. Finalization depends on the key mode. In the \ac{DKG} and council modes, it requires that at least $\theta$ members of the committee holding the key take part; if more than $\nu - \theta$ of them become unavailable, the results are lost, since keys are not reshared to later committees. In the locked \ac{DKG} mode, it also requires the organizer to reveal its secret, and a council whose decryption is opened manually, without a fallback date, depends on its organizer to open it; in both cases, an absent organizer blocks the publication of the results indefinitely. In the sequencer mode, the results depend on the availability of the single key holder.

\paragraph{Quantum resistance.} Quantum computers threaten discrete-logarithm-based cryptography such as ECDSA and ElGamal, and pairing-based proof systems such as Groth16 and PlonK. The \acp{STARK} that prove the batch and results programs are hash-based arguments, made non-interactive with the Fiat--Shamir transform, and are plausibly post-quantum, but their PlonK wrapper, the ballot proofs, the voters' signatures, and the encryption of ballots are not. For this reason, \davinci is designed in a modular way that would allow migration to post-quantum primitives in the longer term. Candidate replacements include lattice-based signature schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA) and Falcon, and the hash-based SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA); lattice-based homomorphic encryption such as BGV or BFV; and the direct on-chain verification of \acp{STARK}.

\paragraph{Verifiability.} Voters can check that their own submissions were recorded, while anyone can audit the entire process to verify the final tally. Every voter can check, with an inclusion proof of their vote identifier against a state root recorded on-chain, that their submission was included in an accepted transition (individual verifiability, in the sense of recorded-as-cast). Since vote identifiers are never removed, this receipt persists after an overwrite, and it does not show by itself that the submission is the ballot that is finally counted; a voter who cast several ballots relies on the ballot settled last prevailing, and on the receiving sequencer settling them in the order it received them, which the protocol does not prove. Additionally, any third party can replay every state transition from the published blobs, check that it reproduces the roots recorded on-chain, and verify the proofs, which confirms that the tally is the sum of the final ballots of the slots (universal verifiability) and that each counted ballot comes with a valid membership proof (eligibility verifiability). The protocol does not provide, by itself, a mechanism for a voter to verify that their device encrypted the intended choice (cast-as-intended verifiability); this property can be added by the client application, for instance with a second device that audits a ballot before it is cast. End-to-end verifiability in the usual sense requires such a mechanism in addition to the properties above.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:analysis:implementation}

\Davinci is implemented as a modular, fully open-source system: the ballot circuit~\cite{davinci-circom}, the \ac{zkVM} programs~\cite{davinci-zkvm}, the smart contracts~\cite{davinci-contracts}, the sequencer node and its client library~\cite{davinci-sequencer}, and the key-management systems~\cite{davinci-dkg,davinci-council} are all publicly available at \url{https://github.com/vocdoni}. Its components can be recombined or integrated with external services through adaptable interfaces, enabling redundancy and integration with third-party applications such as our voting-as-a-service APIs.

\paragraph{Technological stack.}
The ballot circuit is implemented with Circom~\cite{belles2023circom} over BN254 and proven with Groth16~\cite{groth2016size} for its minimal proof size and cheap verification; its ElGamal operations run over BabyJubjub, whose base field matches the BN254 scalar field. The batch and results programs are \texttt{no\_std} Rust programs compiled for the RISC-V target of ZisK~1.3.2-alpha and proven by a GPU prover service, which wraps every proof in PlonK over BN254 for on-chain verification. Inside the programs, SHA-256, Keccak, BN254 pairings and scalar multiplications, SECP256K1 key recovery, BabyJubjub point additions, and 256-bit modular arithmetic run on ZisK precompiles; Poseidon is implemented in software over the BN254 field. The smart contracts are written in Solidity, and the sequencer node and its client library in Rust.

\paragraph{Circuit and program sizes.} The ballot circuit has $64{,}816$ constraints for 16 fields; its proving key is 35.5~MiB and its witness generator 6.5~MiB, which a browser downloads before proving. The execution cost of the batch program depends on its input and grows with the batch. A batch program execution with 512 votes and 512 refreshes takes about $2.3 \cdot 10^8$ steps at $n_f = 2$ and $4.0 \cdot 10^8$ steps at $n_f = 16$. The program accepts batches of up to 1024 votes, 2048 refreshes, and 32 blobs; sequencers currently seal at most 512 votes per batch.

\paragraph{Trusted setup.} Groth16 relies on a circuit-specific common reference string (\ac{CRS}, see Appendix~\ref{sec:cryptographic-primitives:zkp}). The parameters of the ballot circuit must be generated through an \ac{MPC} ceremony in two phases: a universal powers-of-tau phase, for which a public ceremony of $2^{18}$ constraints can be reused, followed by a per-circuit phase that binds the parameters to the constraint system and introduces the secret $\delta$. As long as at least one participant in each phase discards their secret contribution, the resulting \ac{CRS} is secure. The \ac{zkVM} \acp{STARK} need no setup, and their PlonK wrapper uses the universal setup distributed with the ZisK release. The circuits of the \ac{DKG} network and of the council have setups of their own.

\paragraph{Release pins.} A release of the protocol is identified by the values in Table~\ref{tab:pins}, which the registry stores as immutable parameters and sequencers check at startup. A sequencer refuses a registry, a verifier contract, or a prover whose values differ.

\paragraph{Implementation status.} The current implementation includes the ballot circuit, the batch and results programs, the smart contracts, the sequencer node, and both key-management systems, and it runs in production on OP Mainnet in L1-blob mode, with all four key modes enabled. The registry is deployed at \texttt{0x64d41aD49009204E8dAcc153Fc98a5Ee14cB6916}, with a default grace window of 300~s (organizers may choose between 240~s and 600~s), a maximum extension of 1800~s, and a minimum notice of 60~s. The council circuits currently use a development setup produced by a single party, so the soundness of the council's proofs relies on that party having discarded its trapdoor until a multi-party ceremony replaces the setup. No complete independent security audit of the system has been published yet.

\subsection{Performance evaluation}
\label{sec:analysis:performance-evaluation}

We evaluate \davinci along four axes: voter-side proving, sequencer proving, on-chain verification, and data availability, together with the end-to-end throughput of the system. Unless stated otherwise, sequencer measurements use one NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU (32~GB) on a host with an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X3D CPU.

\paragraph{Voter proving.} A ballot proof is generated from the published Groth16 artifacts of the ballot circuit, either in a browser with snarkjs or natively. With the Rust prover of the client library (arkworks), one 16-core CPU produced 8192 ballot proofs in 1326~s, running several proofs in parallel, that is, about six proofs per second and 0.16~s of amortized wall time per proof at that concurrency. We have not measured the latency of browser proving for this version of the circuit; it requires downloading the 35.5~MiB proving key and the 6.5~MiB witness generator, which can be cached across ballots.

\paragraph{Sequencer proving.} Table~\ref{tab:proving} and Fig.~\ref{fig:proving-time} report the time to prove one batch, including witness generation, the \ac{STARK} proof, its recursion, and the PlonK wrapper. The \emph{first} batch of an election has nothing to refresh; a \emph{steady} batch of $n$ new votes refreshes $n$ slots, the regime that determines throughput for fresh votes (a batch of overwrites refreshes up to twice as many slots as it writes, and a re-sealed batch more). A batch of two votes takes about $15.5$~s, mostly the fixed cost of recursion and of the PlonK wrapper, and each refresh adds 13--16~ms at $n_f = 2$ and 40--50~ms at $n_f = 16$, the upper values at the largest batches. Throughput peaks around 512 votes per batch at $n_f = 2$ and 256 at $n_f = 16$; beyond that, the batch size is bounded by the host memory of the prover (about 54~GB for a steady batch of 1024 votes), not by the GPU. The proof is 768 bytes, plus 512 bytes of public values, at every batch size. These figures were measured on ZisK~1.3.0-alpha, the predecessor of the release in Table~\ref{tab:pins}, with the same batch program logic~\cite{davinci-zkvm}.

\paragraph{End-to-end throughput.} With two provers, each serving one sequencer that runs two elections of 2048 voters at $n_f = 2$ in batches of 512, the system proved and settled 8192 votes of four independent elections in $678.7$~s, a sustained throughput of $12.07$ votes per second ($724$ per minute), on a local chain with one-second blocks and without competing sequencers. Each prover was busy for the whole run, so aggregate throughput scales with the number of provers serving independent elections: one RTX 5090 settles about six votes per second at $n_f = 2$. The current sequencer keeps at most one batch in flight per election, so these measurements show scaling across independent elections, not the acceleration of one election by additional provers; settlement within an election remains ordered by state root.

\paragraph{On-chain verification.} Thanks to the succinctness of PlonK, verifying a batch proof costs an essentially constant amount of gas, independent of the number of ballots in the batch; the cost of a settlement grows with the number of blobs, by one point evaluation each, and, in L1-blob mode, with the number of Ethereum headers and transaction proofs it carries. In blob mode, measured with the same contracts on Gnosis Chain (a chain with native blob transactions, on which \davinci was deployed before OP Mainnet), a settlement with one blob costs 0.40--0.44M gas and one with two blobs 0.46--0.50M gas. On OP Mainnet in L1-blob mode, verifying the L1 proof with three Ethereum headers costs 430K--460K gas, and a whole settlement 800K--860K gas.

\paragraph{Data availability.} \Davinci publishes the data needed to reconstruct each state transition through EIP-4844 blobs. Compared with calldata, blobs substantially reduce data-availability costs by separating large protocol data from permanent Ethereum state. A blob holds 4096 cells; a steady batch needs $1 + \lceil 3n/4 \rceil + 4 n_f n$ cells for $n$ new votes and $n$ refreshes, so a batch of 128 votes at $n_f = 2$ fits in a single 128~KiB blob, while 512 votes at $n_f = 16$ need nine. In the measurements on OP Mainnet in L1-blob mode, in October 2026, posting the blobs of a batch cost 1.9--3.7~$\mu$ETH on Ethereum and settling it 0.04--0.05~$\mu$ETH on OP Mainnet, and each settled batch landed 85--115~s after being sealed, a delay dominated by the time the rollup takes to adopt a new Ethereum block as its L1 origin. These measurements used an earlier registry with a 150~s grace floor, under which some batches of 128 and 256 votes at $n_f = 16$ were split in two, so that the whole case finished 200--230~s after its first seal; the current registry's 240~s floor was chosen to let such a batch settle at once.\\

Together, these results indicate that \davinci scales to large elections at predictable settlement costs and practical proving times. Since proving dominates and parallelizes across provers and elections, throughput grows with the proving capacity of the sequencers, while the settlement chain only verifies one constant-size proof per batch.
